How to Price Cookies

Work out your cost per cookie and a fair price per dozen.

Free Recipe Cost Calculator › Pricing guides

Cookies feel cheap to make, so they're one of the easiest things to underprice. But a batch of cookies still takes an hour or two of your time, plus bags, labels and oven time. This guide shows you how to price cookies so you're paid for every dozen, whether you sell classic drop cookies at a farmers market or decorated sugar cookies for parties.

The formula: (ingredients + labor + overhead + packaging) ÷ number of cookies = cost per cookie. Add your profit markup to that to get your price.

Step 1: Add up your ingredients

For each ingredient, divide the amount you used by the package size and multiply by the package price. Here's a worked example for a batch of 24 chocolate chip cookies. The package prices are example prices; use your own receipts.

Ingredient total: $8.23, or about 34¢ per cookie. Notice that the chocolate chips and butter are over two-thirds of the cost. Add-ins like nuts, chunks and premium chocolate change your price a lot, so cost each flavor separately.

Step 2: Count your time honestly

Include mixing, chilling the dough if your recipe needs it, scooping, baking in rounds, cooling, bagging and cleanup. In the example that's 1.5 hours at $18 an hour, or $27 of labor. Decorated sugar cookies are a different story: royal icing work can take several times longer per cookie, so time a real batch before you quote.

Step 3: Overhead and packaging

Use 5–15% of ingredients plus labor for oven power, equipment wear and small supplies. At 10% that's $3.52. Then add packaging: cello bags, twist ties, a box, labels. We'll use $4.00 per batch. Overhead plus packaging: $7.52.

Step 5: Add profit and set your price

With a 30% markup, the example comes to $2.32 per cookie, or about $27.80 a dozen. Rounded, that's $2.25–$2.50 each or $28 a dozen. Decorated, custom-designed cookies usually call for a higher markup because each one is design work, not just baking.

Price by the dozen, not the cookie

  • Set a dozen price and a half-dozen price. Small orders take nearly as much packaging and handling time, so charge a bit more per cookie for them.
  • Set a minimum order for custom decorated cookies, such as one or two dozen, so a tiny order doesn't take a whole evening.
  • Charge for extras: custom designs, individual bagging, specialty flavors, rush orders and delivery.
  • Sell at markets in bundles (for example 3 or 6 per bag) so the math stays simple at the table.

Check the market, then stand by your number

Look at what local bakers charge for comparable cookies. If your cost is higher than the going price, look for speed and bulk buying before cutting your wage. Most customers buying from a home baker are paying for fresh, homemade quality, not a supermarket price.
