How to Price Sourdough Bread

Work out your real cost per loaf and a fair price for homemade sourdough.

Free Recipe Cost Calculator › Pricing guides

Sourdough looks cheap to make: flour, water and salt. But a loaf takes a day or two from feeding the starter to cooling on the rack, it ties up your oven for an hour or more at high heat, and the hands-on steps add up. This guide shows how to price sourdough bread so each loaf covers ingredients, your time, oven costs and profit, with a worked example for a batch of four loaves.

The formula: (ingredients + labor + overhead + packaging) ÷ number of loaves = cost per loaf. Add your profit markup to get your price.

Step 1: Cost the flour, salt and starter

For each ingredient, divide what you used by the package size and multiply by the package price. Don't forget the flour you feed your starter, including what you throw away as discard; it's a real cost of every bake. The package prices below are example prices; use your own receipts. Water is listed at $0 here, but if you use bottled or filtered water, count it.

Ingredient total: $5.50 for four loaves, about $1.38 per loaf. That's why sourdough is easy to underprice: the ingredients really are cheap, so almost the whole price is your time and your oven.

Step 2: Count hands-on time, not waiting time

Most of a sourdough bake is waiting while the dough ferments, and you shouldn't charge an hourly wage for hours you spent asleep or at work. Do count the hands-on steps: feeding the starter, mixing, stretch-and-folds, shaping, scoring, loading and unloading the oven, cooling, bagging, labeling and cleanup. In this example that's 2 hours for four loaves at $18 an hour, or $36.00 of labor. Time a real batch; your number may be higher, especially while you're still learning.

Step 3: Overhead (higher than usual) and packaging

Many bakers use 5–15% of ingredients plus labor for overhead. Sourdough belongs at the high end, because the oven is often preheated with a Dutch oven inside and then runs at high heat for each bake, and Dutch ovens, baskets and lames wear out. At 15% that's $6.22. For packaging, a bread bag and a label run about 75¢ a loaf, or $3.00 for four.

Step 5: Add profit and set your price

With a 30% markup, the example comes to $16.48 per loaf. Rounded up to a clean number, that's about $17 for a plain sourdough loaf. Inclusions like cheese, jalapeños, olives, seeds or chocolate add ingredient cost and time, so price those loaves separately.

Check your local market

Look at what bakeries, farmers market vendors and other home bakers near you charge for a comparable loaf, and compare loaf weight too, since sizes vary a lot. If your price is well above what your market pays, the biggest lever is batch size: if your oven fits two Dutch ovens, or you bake back-to-back, the hands-on time per loaf drops quickly. Baking only one or two loaves at a time is what makes sourdough expensive. Fix it there before you cut your hourly rate.

Pricing tips for sourdough bread

  • Bake in batches on a set schedule, for example one bake day a week, and take pre-orders by a cutoff so you know how many loaves to mix.
  • Price add-in loaves separately. A cheddar-jalapeño loaf costs more to make than a plain one.
  • Sell your extras. Discard crackers or a small jar of starter can use up what you'd otherwise throw away; cost them like any other product.
  • Re-check flour prices. Flour is most of your ingredient cost, so a price change on a 5 lb bag shows up in every loaf.
  • Check your cottage food rules and label allergens. Bread is commonly allowed under cottage food laws, but labeling rules vary by state, and wheat is a major allergen.
