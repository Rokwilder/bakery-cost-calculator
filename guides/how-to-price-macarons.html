How to Price Macarons

Find your real cost per macaron and a fair price per box.

Free Recipe Cost Calculator › Pricing guides

Macarons are small, but they're one of the most time-consuming things a home baker can sell. The shells are finicky, a humid day or an uneven oven can ruin a tray, and every pair is piped, matched, filled and boxed by hand. This guide shows how to price macarons so each one covers ingredients, labor, failed shells and profit, with a worked example for 24 filled macarons.

The formula: (ingredients + labor + overhead + packaging) ÷ number of sellable macarons = cost per macaron. Add your profit markup to get your price.

Step 1: Cost the shells and the filling

For each ingredient, divide what you used by the package size and multiply by the package price. Almond flour is usually the most expensive ingredient. The package prices below are example prices; use your own receipts. This example uses a simple vanilla buttercream filling.

Step 2: Plan for cracked and hollow shells

Almost every macaron baker loses some shells to cracks, hollows, lopsided feet or mismatched sizes. Don't hide that loss. Pipe a few extra shells, and divide your costs by the number of macarons you can actually sell, not the number you hoped to make. In this example, the batch is piped for a few extra and the cost is divided by 24 sellable macarons. If you regularly lose a whole tray, make the batch bigger on paper or raise the price until your success rate improves.

Step 3: Count your time

Include separating and weighing, sifting, whipping the meringue, macaronage, piping, resting the shells, baking in batches, cooling, matching pairs, making and piping the filling, and boxing and cleanup. In this example that's 3.5 hours at $18 an hour, or $63.00 of labor. Many bakers fill macarons and let them rest in the fridge overnight before selling; don't charge an hourly wage for that resting time, but do plan for it when you set pickup dates.

Step 4: Overhead and packaging

Use 5–15% of ingredients plus labor for oven power, equipment wear and small supplies like parchment or silicone mats and piping bags. At 10% that's $6.92. Macarons need sturdy packaging: four 6-count macaron boxes with inserts at about $1.25 each come to $5.00.

Step 6: Add profit and set your price

Macarons are a specialty item, so a higher markup makes sense. With a 40% markup, the example comes to $4.73 per macaron, or about $28.38 for a box of 6. Rounded up, that's about $4.75 each, or $28.50 for a box of 6 and $57.00 a dozen. Custom colors, painted or printed designs, and character shapes take longer, so charge more for them.

Check your local market

See what bakeries, coffee shops and other home bakers near you charge for macarons, and compare size and filling. If your price is far above the market, look at your time and your failure rate first: bigger batches and a reliable recipe lower your cost per macaron much more than cheaper ingredients do. Frozen grocery-store macarons are a different product from fresh, filled-to-order ones, so they're not the right comparison.

Pricing tips for macarons

  • Sell by the box, in 6s and 12s, and set a minimum per flavor. Mixing a color for 6 macarons takes nearly as long as for 24.
  • Charge for custom work: matching party colors, painted details, printed images and character shapes.
  • Limit flavors per order, or charge extra for each additional flavor, since every one needs its own filling.
  • Book ahead and take a deposit. Macarons can need a day to rest after filling, and custom colors can't be resold.
  • Check your cottage food rules. Fillings that need refrigeration, such as cream cheese frosting or curds, may not be allowed in some states. Check before you add them to your menu.
